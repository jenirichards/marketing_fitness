“Under your own name” does two jobs. It states the offer and it retires the phrase white-label in the same breath – see the note below. The headline had to grow from “an app” to cover website, app and portal without becoming a list; “your whole club online” carries all three, and the second line is the part a club owner repeats to their business partner.

On “white-label”. It is industry jargon that describes the arrangement from our side of the table, and to a gym owner who has not met it before it sounds like they are being sold something generic with their sticker on it – the opposite of the truth. Four replacements worth considering: “under your own name” (used throughout here – plain, and it doubles as headline copy); “own-brand” (British retail language, understood instantly, and it carries the right pride); “your brand, start to finish”; or simply never naming the concept and letting the four club icons make the point. The one thing not to do is keep a term the buyer has to decode.

Home · why clubs do this

A section the site does not have at all. It goes before a single feature is mentioned, because a club owner has to want the outcome before they will read about an exercise library.

harbourfitness.co.uk#why
Why clubs do this

Members don't leave because
the training was bad.

They leave because nothing ever showed them it was working. The members who log their sessions, watch their numbers move, and feel a coach paying attention are the ones still turning up in month six.

The wins go unseen
Six weeks of hard-won progress lives in the coach's head and on a paper card. The member never sees the line going up – so they never feel it. Nothing to be proud of, nothing to show a friend, nothing to stay for.
You're guessing at drift
You can feel who's slipping, but you can't see it. By the time you notice someone hasn't been in for three weeks, the retention conversation is already a month late – and usually over.
Someone else's logo
Your members finish your session and open a stranger's app to record it. That app gets the habit, the data and the credit for their progress. Your name is on the door and nowhere else.

No invented statistics. The obvious way to write this section is with retention percentages, and there is no source for any that would survive a club owner asking where the number came from. So it argues from the reader's own experience instead: every one of these three is something they have already watched happen. It costs nothing in force and it cannot be challenged.

The worked example is doing most of the work here. Per-member pricing is hard to feel – a club owner reads “£2.50 per member” and either under-estimates it or panics. One arithmetic block with a real headcount turns it into a single number they can hold against what they already spend. The three lines are drawn in descending commitment order: the once, the fixed, then the variable, so the smallest and most alarming number arrives last, already framed.

“Every feature anyone asks for” is the strongest sentence on the page and it is currently buried in a bullet. No competitor at this price can say it, because they are selling seats on a fixed product. It says the club is buying a relationship, not a licence – and it quietly explains why there are no tiers. Worth testing as the pricing headline in its own right, and worth being sure you are happy to be held to it.

Decided: Option B, Ascent. It is the braver of the two I put forward - it draws the promise of the product rather than the equipment, and it gives up the initial to do it. It is now live on both sites: the favicon and apple-touch icon, the nav and footer lockups, the OG card, and the Harbour Fitness tile on the studio site. One implementation note worth knowing - the nav and footer render the mark as inline SVG with the wordmark as real HTML text rather than loading the lockup file through <img>, because an SVG loaded that way is an isolated document and cannot reach the page's webfonts; the wordmark would have quietly fallen back to Arial while the rest of the site sat in Geist. The .svg files are still what the favicon, the OG card and anything outside the site use.
All four are in the repo as install-ready SVGs docs/logos/ – each as a 64×64 app icon and a 320×64 horizontal lockup. To adopt one, copy it over public/logos/harbourFitnessIcon.svg and harbourFitnessLogo.svg. Two caveats: the lockups set the wordmark as SVG <text>, exactly as the current logo does, so it renders with a fallback face on any machine without the display font – convert to outlines for production. And the tiles are a flat navy rather than the current gradient, because flat holds up better at favicon size. All eight have been re-cut in amber to match the accent you chose.

Notes and open points

What drawing the page resolved, and what it left unresolved.

Resolved here

  • The offer was being under-sold by half. The live site sells an app. The product is a club's website, its own-name apps, and the portal that drives all of it – and the website is the half that brings new members in. Adding it changed the headline, the timeline, the comparison band and the price, so it was not a section to bolt on; it moved the whole page.
  • “White-label” is gone. It describes the arrangement from our side of the table and it sounds generic to the person buying. “Under your own name” replaces it and doubles as the second line of the headline. Alternatives are set out in the note under the hero if that one doesn't sit right.
  • One system, not three, is the argument that justifies the price. Any club can buy a website; any club can buy a booking tool. The band that follows one member from a Tuesday booking through to logging sets in the app is the only place that says why this is different, and it earned its own section.
  • The pricing is one offer, not a tier table. With tiers gone there was nothing left to columnise, and the worked example does the persuading: per-member pricing is impossible to feel until somebody does the arithmetic for you at your own headcount.
  • “Every feature anyone asks for” turned out to be the strongest claim on the page. It explains the absence of tiers, it cannot be matched by anyone selling seats on a fixed product, and it reframes the monthly fee as a relationship rather than a licence.
  • The portal drawing was three releases out of date. Memberships, purchases, payments and the Top Lifts board are all new since the marketing copy was written. Top Lifts in particular – a member's name against the heaviest lift in each exercise – is a retention feature disguised as a stat card, and it had never been mentioned anywhere.
  • The ground was too dark, and it was too dark for a reason worth naming. It was #070d1a, a near-black. That is the house style of developer-tool marketing – it is our aesthetic, not a gym's – and paired with hard geometry it codes to strength gyms and CrossFit boxes rather than to the small-group, wellness and women's training studios that are actually buying. The ground is now #0f1f3d, the same navy as this page's header, and every value that changes between versions was pulled into one block so the whole page can flip.
  • The club owns the member data, and that now has a place on the page. It answers the question every owner asks, and it matters more than it used to: the website, the bookings and the card payments all run through us, so what a prospect is really asking about is their customer list and their revenue, not workout logs. It sits in the ownership band rather than buried in an FAQ, because it is the strongest version of the argument that band is already making – and it closes the loop on the “someone else's logo” card further up the page, which complains that a generic app takes the habit and the data.
  • Two features were being given away for free. The how did it feel and difficulty ratings on the log screen appear nowhere on the site. They are the most distinctive thing in the app and they are the coach's argument, not the member's.

Still open

  • Amber against the club tiles. Amber is now the default and it is the right call – it is warmer, it suits a fitness brand, and it is already the accent the product's own harbour brand carries. The one risk is that it is a warm colour sitting near warm club brands: Ironline's red is a near neighbour. The mitigation here is structural rather than lucky – the club tiles only ever appear inside the dark navy panel, where the amber never goes. Worth re-checking the first time a club turns up with an orange logo. Switch to teal at the top of the page to compare.
  • “So can I have a copy?” is the very next question. The page now says the club owns its data, which is the right claim and a strong one. A prospect asks the follow-up in the same breath: can I export it, and what happens to it if I leave? I have not answered that on the page because I do not know the answer. Whatever it is, it is worth deciding before a prospect asks it in a demo – and if the answer is a clean one, it is worth saying out loud rather than waiting to be asked.
  • There is still no customer quote. You said you would get one. The spotlight section is drawn assuming it exists; one real sentence from Sam about what having her own site and app changed would be worth more than any other single addition to this page.
  • Sam's real prices and programme names are on this page club-sites/samwilsonzara/data/programmes.ts. Fine for an internal mockup; before it ships either get her blessing or swap them for generic examples.
  • Ironline needs its artwork either way. The mark drawn here is a proposal. The demo club currently falls back to Harbour's icon, so any demo build shows the wrong logo – which is a poor advert for a product whose pitch is that the branding is yours. If the mark works, it wants dropping into brands/ironline/ as real artwork.
  • The display typeface. This page uses the system stack so it stays a single standalone file, but the specification is a tighter, more athletic face than Geist at these sizes – something in the Archivo / Söhne register, tracked to about −0.035em with the leading pulled under 1.0. At 76px the face is most of the impression.
  • Navy or light – light is now the default, provisionally. All three versions are on the switcher at the top, and the page opens on light because that is where the argument lands – but it is not settled until somebody in the target market has looked at it. Sam is the right person to ask, and it is a good excuse for the conversation where you also ask her for the quote. The light version is not a tint of the navy one: it is an inversion, so the page keeps exactly the same rhythm and the same number of changes of ground, and the bands that were dark become white while the band that was white becomes navy. The club tileset and the closing panel stay dark in both, because the icons and the final call to action each need something to sit on.

    My reading is that light wins, on three grounds. The product screenshots are light UIs – on navy they float as bright rectangles, on white they sit in the page and get looked at, and the product is the best argument here. A club owner is evaluating this on a laptop during a working day, where light reads as a business tool and dark reads as a brand asking to be admired. And the near-black-and-geometry look is so standard in software marketing now that it says “built by developers” more loudly than it says anything about training. Navy's case is that it has more presence and is more distinctive in a category of white SaaS pages – which is real, and is why both are still drawn rather than one deleted.

    The navy version is not wasted either way. The thing it says – built by developers, with presence – is exactly right for the parent studio's own site, where the audience is people buying software and the aesthetic is the credential rather than the obstacle. Worth holding on to for Harbour SW rather than throwing away.
